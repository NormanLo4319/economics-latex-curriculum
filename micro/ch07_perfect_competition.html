7  Chapter 7: Perfect Competition and Long-Run Industry Equilibrium

This chapter provides a rigorous, algebra-first treatment of perfect competition, benchmarked to Eric Chiang’s Microeconomics. It examines price-taking behavior and short-run profit maximization, short-run shutdown versus long-run exit decisions, and the dynamics of free entry and exit that drive competitive industries toward zero economic profit and full efficiency in the long run.

TipBring It Home: Why Did the Food-Truck Boom Cool Down—While Some Trucks Kept Rolling Through Slow Winters?

Suppose a wave of gourmet taco trucks opens in a major U.S. city. Early operators sell out every afternoon and earn generous profits, inspiring dozens of new entrepreneurs to outfit trucks of their own. Within two years, street corners are crowded, lunch prices level off, and owners complain they are barely breaking even''—yet most trucks stay in business. Meanwhile, during a rainy winter month when daily revenue fails to cover monthly truck loans and commissary rent, some operators keep serving lunch while others park their trucks until spring. Why do early profits vanish so quickly in open markets, why does a money-losing truck sometimes stay open, and why would anyone remain in an industry earningzero economic profit’’? By the end of this chapter, you will be able to answer all three questions using the competitive model.

7.1 Price-Taking Behavior and Short-Run Profit Maximization

Conceptual Introduction

Imagine a family corn farm in Iowa ready to sell its autumn harvest at a local grain elevator. Even if the farm harvests tens of thousands of bushels, it represents a microscopic share of global grain production and cannot negotiate its own price—it sells at the prevailing commodity spot price. Economists model markets like this using perfect competition, an idealized benchmark defined by four strict assumptions: (1) many small buyers and sellers, (2) identical (homogeneous) products across every firm, (3) perfect information about prices and quality, and (4) free entry and exit in the long run with zero barriers. Agricultural commodities such as wheat, corn, and soybeans come closest to this benchmark in practice.

Because each competitive firm supplies a tiny fraction of total market output and sells an identical product, an individual producer has zero power to raise price above the going market rate—if a farmer charges even one penny more, buyers switch instantly to neighboring sellers. Conversely, there is no reason to cut price below the market rate because the firm can already sell all it wishes at the going price. Consequently, every competitive firm is a price taker facing a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve at the market equilibrium price \(P^*\). Every additional unit sold adds exactly \(P^*\) to Total Revenue (\(TR = P^* \times q\)), meaning Price equals Marginal Revenue and Average Revenue (\(P^* = MR = AR\)).

To maximize economic profit (\(\pi = TR - TC\)), a competitive firm expands output one unit at a time as long as the extra revenue from selling another unit exceeds the extra cost of producing it (\(MR > MC\)). It stops at the exact quantity \(q^*\) where Marginal Revenue equals Marginal Cost on the upward-sloping portion of the \(MC\) curve:

\[ P^* = MR = MC(q^*) \]

Once optimal output \(q^*\) is chosen, the firm’s profit or loss per unit is the vertical gap between market price \(P^*\) and Average Total Cost (\(ATC\)) at \(q^*\), giving total economic profit \(\pi = (P^* - ATC) \times q^*\). When global grain prices surge due to droughts elsewhere, an individual farmer’s horizontal \(P = MR\) line shifts upward, prompting the farmer to apply extra inputs and expand output along \(MC\) to capture positive short-run economic profits.

NoteClear It Up: Market Demand Slopes Down, Yet the Firm’s Demand Is Horizontal

Students often wonder how market demand can obey the Law of Demand (sloping downward) while each competitive firm faces a flat demand curve. The resolution is scale: in the entire market, lower prices are required to persuade consumers to buy millions of extra bushels. For a single small farm, however, expanding output from \(50\) to \(100\) bushels is a drop in the ocean—too small to move the market price noticeably—so the individual firm takes \(P^*\) as fixed.

Mathematical Foundation (Algebra Level)

Assuming the firm is a price taker with a rising Marginal Cost schedule and operates above its shutdown threshold (\(P^* \ge \min AVC\)), profit maximization occurs at \(P^* = MC(q^*)\), generating total economic profit:

\[ \pi = TR - TC = (P^* - ATC) \times q^* \]

NoteWork It Out: Short-Run Profit Maximization for a Blueberry Grower

Problem: An illustrative competitive blueberry grower faces a market price of \(P^* = \$30\) per crate. The grower’s Total Cost, Average Variable Cost, and Marginal Cost schedules are:

\[ TC(q) = 48 + 6q + 2q^2, \qquad AVC(q) = 6 + 2q, \qquad MC(q) = 6 + 4q \]

Find the profit-maximizing quantity of crates \(q^*\), Average Total Cost at \(q^*\), and total economic profit \(\pi\).

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Apply the Profit-Maximization Rule (\(P^* = MC\)): Set market price equal to Marginal Cost and solve for \(q^*\):

\[ 30 = 6 + 4q^* \implies 24 = 4q^* \implies q^* = \mathbf{6\text{ crates}} \]

Check that the firm covers variable costs: at \(q^* = 6\), \(AVC(6) = 6 + 2(6) = \$18 < \$30\), so operating is profitable.

  • Calculate Total Cost (\(TC\)) and Average Total Cost (\(ATC\)) at \(q^* = 6\):

\[ TC(6) = 48 + 6(6) + 2(6^2) = 48 + 36 + 72 = \$156 \]

\[ ATC(6) = \frac{\$156}{6} = \mathbf{\$26.00\text{ per crate}} \]

  • Calculate Economic Profit (\(\pi\)) Two Ways: Using Total Revenue (\(TR = \$30 \times 6 = \$180\)) minus Total Cost:

\[ \pi = TR - TC = \$180 - \$156 = \mathbf{+\$24} \]

Cross-checking with the profit-rectangle formula:

\[ \pi = (P^* - ATC) \times q^* = (\$30 - \$26) \times 6 = \$4 \times 6 = \mathbf{+\$24} \]

Conclusion: Producing \(6\) crates per period maximizes short-run economic profit at \(+\$24\).

NoteOptional Advanced Section: Calculus Conditions for Profit Maximization

Note for Economics Majors and Quantitative Students: A competitive firm chooses \(q \ge 0\) to maximize \(\pi(q) = P^* q - TC(q)\). The first-order condition (FOC) requires:

\[ \frac{d\pi}{dq} = P^* - \frac{d(TC)}{dq} = P^* - MC(q) = 0 \implies P^* = MC(q^*) \]

The second-order condition (SOC) for a local maximum requires:

\[ \frac{d^2\pi}{dq^2} = -\frac{d(MC)}{dq} < 0 \implies \frac{d(MC)}{dq} > 0 \]

Thus, profit is maximized only where \(P^* = MC\) along the upward-sloping segment of the Marginal Cost curve. For the blueberry grower above, \(TC(q) = 48 + 6q + 2q^2\) yields \(MC(q) = 6 + 4q\) and \(MC'(q) = 4 > 0\), confirming that \(q^* = 6\) is a unique global maximum.

7.2 Short-Run Shutdown vs. Long-Run Exit Rules

Conceptual Introduction

Consider an illustrative beachfront hotel in a seasonal tourist town that faces high fixed mortgage and insurance bills year-round. In November, off-peak room rates fall from \(\$280\) to \(\$95\) per night, far below the hotel’s full average cost per room. Yet the variable cost of housekeeping and utilities for an occupied room is only \(\$45\) per night. Because \(P = \$95\) exceeds \(AVC = \$45\), each occupied room contributes \(\$50\) toward paying the unavoidable mortgage, so staying open loses less money than locking the doors for the autumn.

Many students mistakenly assume that any money-losing business (\(\pi < 0\)) should immediately close. That intuition misses the crucial distinction between sunk fixed costs in the short run and escapable fixed costs in the long run:

  • Short-Run Shutdown Rule (\(P < \min AVC\)): In the short run, fixed costs (such as a signed building lease or property mortgage) must be paid even when output is zero. As long as market price covers Average Variable Cost (\(P \ge \min AVC\)), every unit sold pays for its workers and materials plus contributes cash toward fixed overhead. The firm should keep operating at a loss to minimize its deficit. Only when \(P < \min AVC\) does operating lose even more money than shutting down.
  • Long-Run Exit Rule (\(P < \min ATC\)): In the long run, leases expire and all costs become variable and avoidable. If market price remains persistently below minimum Average Total Cost (\(P < \min ATC\)), the firm cannot cover its full economic costs and will permanently exit the industry.

Consequently, a competitive firm supplies \(q = 0\) whenever \(P < \min AVC\) and produces along \(P = MC\) whenever \(P \ge \min AVC\). Therefore, the firm’s short-run supply curve is the upward-sloping portion of its Marginal Cost curve lying at or above \(\min AVC\).

NoteClear It Up: Shutdown Is Temporary; Exit Is Permanent

A shutdown is a short-run decision to produce \(q = 0\) today while remaining in the industry and still paying fixed overhead (like a ski resort closing its lifts in July while continuing to pay property taxes and loan payments). Exit is a long-run decision to leave the market permanently, terminate leases, sell equipment, and eliminate all fixed and variable costs.

Visual Representation

Figure 2 derives \(AVC\), \(ATC\), and \(MC\) from a single consistent cubic cost structure so that \(MC\) intersects both \(AVC\) and \(ATC\) at their exact mathematical minima, mapping the three short-run decision zones.

Mathematical Foundation (Algebra Level)

Assuming fixed cost \(FC\) is sunk in the short run, comparing profit from operating (\(\pi_{\text{operate}} = TR - VC - FC\)) against profit from shutting down (\(\pi_{\text{shutdown}} = -FC\)) yields:

\[ \pi_{\text{operate}} \ge \pi_{\text{shutdown}} \iff TR - VC - FC \ge -FC \iff TR \ge VC \iff P \ge AVC \]

Because a firm only chooses an interior output where \(P = MC\) on the rising segment of \(MC\), operating beats shutting down whenever \(P \ge \min AVC\).

NoteWork It Out: Short-Run Shutdown vs. Long-Run Exit for a Print Shop

Problem: An illustrative small print shop pays daily fixed rent of \(FC = \$300\). Suppose a downturn pushes the competitive market price down to \(P = \$10.00\) per print job. At its profit-maximizing (loss-minimizing) output of \(q^* = 50\) print jobs per day (where \(P = MC = \$10.00\)), its Total Variable Cost is \(VC = \$400\), so \(AVC = \$8.00\) per job and \(ATC = \$14.00\) per job. Calculate the shop’s daily loss if it operates versus its loss if it shuts down, and determine the optimal short-run and long-run decisions.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Calculate Loss If the Shop Operates (\(q^* = 50\)):

\[ TR = \$10.00 \times 50 = \$500, \qquad TC = FC + VC = \$300 + \$400 = \$700 \]

\[ \pi_{\text{operate}} = TR - TC = \$500 - \$700 = \mathbf{-\$200\text{ daily loss}} \]

  • Calculate Loss If the Shop Shuts Down (\(q = 0\)): Revenue is \(\$0\) and variable cost is \(\$0\), leaving the fixed rent unpaid:

\[ \pi_{\text{shutdown}} = \$0 - \$300 = \mathbf{-\$300\text{ daily loss}} \]

  • Compare Short-Run and Long-Run Thresholds: Because \(P\ (\$10.00) > AVC\ (\$8.00)\), every print job contributes \(\$2.00\) toward fixed rent, shrinking the daily loss by \(\$2.00 \times 50 = \$100\) (from \(-\$300\) to \(-\$200\)). However, because the shop still loses money even at its optimal output \(q^* = 50\) (\(P = \$10.00 < ATC = \$14.00\)), the market price necessarily lies below \(\min ATC\).

Conclusion: The shop should stay open in the short run (losing \(\$200\) instead of \(\$300\)), but exit in the long run when its lease expires unless market prices recover to at least \(\min ATC\).

NoteOptional Advanced Section: Why \(MC\) Cuts \(AVC\) and \(ATC\) at Their Minima

Note for Economics Majors and Quantitative Students: Applying the quotient rule to \(AVC(q) = \frac{VC(q)}{q}\) gives:

\[ \frac{d(AVC)}{dq} = \frac{q \cdot \frac{d(VC)}{dq} - VC(q)}{q^2} = \frac{MC(q) - AVC(q)}{q} \]

Setting \(\frac{d(AVC)}{dq} = 0\) implies \(MC(q) = AVC(q)\) at the exact minimum of \(AVC\). By the identical derivation on \(ATC(q) = \frac{TC(q)}{q}\), \(\frac{d(ATC)}{dq} = \frac{MC(q) - ATC(q)}{q} = 0\) implies \(MC(q) = ATC(q)\) at the exact minimum of \(ATC\). Hence the short-run shutdown point \((q_{\text{SD}}, \min AVC)\) and long-run break-even point \((q_{\text{BE}}, \min ATC)\) always lie on the \(MC\) curve.

7.3 Long-Run Industry Equilibrium and Zero Economic Profit

Conceptual Introduction

When craft breweries and gourmet food trucks surged in popularity, early operators earned substantial economic profits. Because startup barriers were modest, hundreds of new entrants opened across major cities within a few years—competing prices downward and dividing customer traffic until typical operators earned a normal return on their labor and equipment investment.

In the long run, free entry and exit act as an automatic self-correcting thermostat for perfectly competitive industries. Whenever existing firms earn positive economic profits (\(\pi > 0\)), entrepreneurs enter the market, shifting the short-run industry supply curve rightward and driving market price down until economic profits vanish. Conversely, if firms suffer economic losses (\(\pi < 0\)), producers exit when leases and equipment contracts expire, shifting industry supply leftward and pushing prices back up until remaining firms break even.

Consequently, a perfectly competitive industry settles into a long-run equilibrium characterized by the triple equality:

\[ P_{\text{LR}} = MR = MC = \min ATC \]

At this equilibrium, firms earn zero economic profit, meaning they earn a normal accounting profit covering all explicit bills plus the owner’s full implicit opportunity cost of time and invested capital. Because each firm operates at the lowest possible average cost (\(P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC\)) and produces the exact quantity where consumers’ marginal willingness to pay equals marginal cost (\(P_{\text{LR}} = MC\)), long-run competitive equilibrium achieves both productive efficiency and allocative efficiency. In a constant-cost industry (where input prices do not change as the industry expands), the long-run industry supply curve (\(S_{\text{LR}}\)) is horizontal at \(P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC\); in an increasing-cost industry (where expansion bids up specialized input prices), \(S_{\text{LR}}\) slopes upward, whereas in a decreasing-cost industry (where external economies lower input prices), \(S_{\text{LR}}\) slopes downward.

NoteClear It Up: Zero Economic Profit Does Not Mean Zero Accounting Profit

``Zero economic profit’’ sounds bleak only if you confuse economic profit with accounting profit. Suppose a food-truck owner earns \(\$300{,}000\) in annual revenue, pays \(\$210{,}000\) in explicit food, fuel, and permit costs, and could earn \(\$80{,}000\) managing a restaurant plus \(\$10{,}000\) in interest on invested savings. Her accounting profit is \(+\$90{,}000\), while her economic profit is \(\$90{,}000 - (\$80{,}000 + \$10{,}000) = \$0\). She is earning exactly her full opportunity cost—a healthy normal return—so she has no incentive to exit, and outsiders have no incentive to rush in.

Visual Representation

Figure 3 illustrates long-run adjustment in a constant-cost competitive industry: positive profits at \(P_1\) attract new firms, shifting industry supply from \(S_1\) to \(S_2\) until market price falls to \(P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC\), eliminating economic profit.

Mathematical Foundation (Algebra Level)

Assuming a constant-cost industry with identical firms and free entry and exit, long-run market price equals each firm’s minimum Average Total Cost, and the equilibrium number of firms \(N^*\) divides total market demand by each firm’s efficient scale \(q_{\text{LR}}\):

\[ P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC, \qquad N^* = \frac{Q_{\text{market}}(P_{\text{LR}})}{q_{\text{LR}}} \]

NoteWork It Out: Long-Run Industry Equilibrium in Lawn Care

Problem: Suppose a city’s competitive lawn-care industry is a constant-cost market in which every firm has identical costs, with minimum Average Total Cost occurring at \(q_{\text{LR}} = 25\) lawns per week and \(\min ATC = \$40\) per lawn. Total weekly market demand for lawn care is linear: \(Q_d = 2{,}000 - 15P\). Find the long-run equilibrium price \(P_{\text{LR}}\), total market quantity \(Q_{\text{LR}}\), and equilibrium number of lawn-care firms \(N^*\).

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Identify Long-Run Equilibrium Price (\(P_{\text{LR}}\)): Free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero, so price must equal minimum Average Total Cost:

\[ P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC = \mathbf{\$40\text{ per lawn}} \]

  • Calculate Total Market Quantity (\(Q_{\text{LR}}\)) at \(P_{\text{LR}} = \$40\): Substitute \(P_{\text{LR}} = 40\) into the market demand equation:

\[ Q_{\text{LR}} = 2{,}000 - 15(40) = 2{,}000 - 600 = \mathbf{1{,}400\text{ lawns per week}} \]

  • Calculate Equilibrium Number of Firms (\(N^*\)): Divide total market output by each firm’s efficient scale (\(q_{\text{LR}} = 25\)):

\[ N^* = \frac{Q_{\text{LR}}}{q_{\text{LR}}} = \frac{1{,}400}{25} = \mathbf{56\text{ firms}} \]

Conclusion: In long-run equilibrium, \(56\) identical firms each service \(25\) lawns per week at \(\$40\) per lawn, earning zero economic profit (a normal accounting return).

NoteOptional Advanced Section: Calculus Derivation of Long-Run Supply

Note for Economics Majors and Quantitative Students: Suppose each firm in a constant-cost competitive industry has Total Cost \(TC(q) = 100 + 20q + q^2\). Marginal Cost is \(MC(q) = \frac{d(TC)}{dq} = 20 + 2q\), and Average Total Cost is \(ATC(q) = \frac{100}{q} + 20 + q\). Setting \(MC(q) = ATC(q)\) locates minimum \(ATC\):

\[ 20 + 2q = \frac{100}{q} + 20 + q \implies q = \frac{100}{q} \implies q^2 = 100 \implies q_{\text{LR}} = 10 \]

Substituting \(q_{\text{LR}} = 10\) into \(MC(q)\) (or \(ATC(q)\)) gives \(P_{\text{LR}} = 20 + 2(10) = \$40\), establishing a horizontal long-run industry supply curve at \(P_{\text{LR}} = \$40\).

TipBring It Home: Closing the Loop on the Food-Truck Market

We can now explain every puzzle from the chapter’s opening food-truck story using the three competitive rules:

  • Why early profits vanished: Low startup barriers allowed new trucks to enter whenever \(P > \min ATC\), shifting market supply rightward until price settled at \(P_{\text{LR}} = \min ATC\).
  • Why some trucks kept serving through slow winters: Truck loan payments and annual commissary leases are sunk short-run fixed costs. Any truck whose winter lunch price still exceeded its variable cost of ingredients and hourly staff (\(P \ge \min AVC\)) minimized its loss by staying open, whereas trucks with \(P < \min AVC\) shut down temporarily until spring.
  • Why owners stay at ``zero economic profit’’: Zero economic profit means truck owners are covering every explicit expense plus earning a full normal return on their own time and invested capital—so staying in business is just as rewarding as their next-best alternative.
